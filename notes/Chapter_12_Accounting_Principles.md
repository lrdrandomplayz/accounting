## 2. Why do we need rules?

Every business prepares financial statements. If each business used its own rules, nobody could compare them. Owners, banks, investors and tax authorities need information which is reliable and prepared the same way. Accounting concepts are the **basic rules** everyone follows.

International Accounting Standards (IAS) build on these concepts so statements from different businesses and countries are prepared in a similar way.

## 3. The ten concepts

### 3.1 Business entity

The business is **separate** from its owner. Only business transactions are recorded.

* Example: the owner's private house and family shopping are not in the business books.
* When the owner takes goods or money for private use, record it as **drawings**.

### 3.2 Duality (double entry)

Every transaction has **two effects**: one debit and one credit of equal value.

* Example: buying equipment for $5,000 by cheque. Equipment rises (debit), bank falls (credit).
* This is why Assets = Capital + Liabilities always balances.

### 3.3 Money measurement

Only items which can be measured **in money** are recorded.

* Example: staff skill, customer loyalty and a good location are valuable, but they are not recorded because they have no reliable money value.

### 3.4 Historical cost

Assets are recorded at their **original cost**, not their current market value.

* Example: land bought for $50,000 ten years ago is still recorded at $50,000, even if it is now worth $90,000.
* Cost is a fact supported by an invoice, so it is **objective**.

### 3.5 Going concern

Assume the business will **continue** trading for the foreseeable future.

* Example: non-current assets are shown at cost less depreciation, not at the amount they would fetch in a closing-down sale.
* If the business is about to close, assets are valued at what they would sell for.

### 3.6 Realisation

Revenue is recorded when it is **earned**, which is when goods or services pass to the customer. It is not when the cash is received.

* Example: goods sold on credit on 28 December and paid for on 15 January are this year's revenue.

### 3.7 Accruals (matching)

Match income and expenses to the **period they belong to**, whether or not cash has been paid or received.

* Example: December electricity paid in January is an expense of December's year. (Chapter 11.)
* Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over the years it is used. (Chapter 10.)

### 3.8 Consistency

Once a method is chosen, **use it every year**. Change only for a good reason, and explain the change.

* Example: if vehicles use reducing balance depreciation at 25%, use the same method next year. This lets you compare one year with another.

### 3.9 Prudence

Do not **overstate** assets or profit. Do not **understate** liabilities or losses. Record losses as soon as they are likely; record profits only when they are earned.

* Example: create an allowance for irrecoverable debts. (Chapter 8.)
* Example: value inventory at the **lower of cost and net realisable value**.

### 3.10 Materiality

Small (immaterial) items do not need strict treatment if the result would not affect a user's decision.

* Example: a $5 stapler will last several years, but it is recorded as an expense rather than a non-current asset. It is not worth depreciating.
* What is material depends on the size of the business.

| Concept | One line meaning | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| Business entity | Business and owner are separate | Drawings, capital |
| Duality | Every transaction has two sides | Double entry, trial balance |

## 5. Useful information: the four qualities

Accounting information must be useful. It should have these qualities:

| Quality | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Helps users make decisions. It is up to date and on time. |
| Reliability | Free from errors and bias. It can be checked (for example against invoices). |
| Comparability | Can be compared with earlier years and with other businesses. Consistency helps this. |
| Understandability | Clear and set out so users with reasonable knowledge can follow it. |

## 6. Inventory valuation (links to prudence)

Inventory is valued at the **lower** of:

* **Cost:** what the business paid, plus carriage inwards.
* **Net realisable value (NRV):** expected selling price minus any costs to sell it (repairs, selling costs).

Example: goods cost $800. They are damaged. They can be sold for $700 after $50 of repairs. NRV = 700 - 50 = 650. Value the goods at **$650**.

## 7. Applying concepts: how to answer questions

When asked "Which concept applies?":

1. Read the situation.
2. Ask: is it about **who** (entity), **when** (realisation, accruals), **how much** (historical cost, prudence, materiality), **method** (consistency), **future** (going concern), or **measurement** (money measurement)?
3. Name the concept **and** explain it in one sentence linked to the situation.

### Worked example 12.1

"The owner took $400 of goods from the shop for her family."

* Concept: **Business entity.**
* Explanation: the business and the owner are separate, so the goods are recorded as drawings, not as a business expense.

### Worked example 12.2

"A trader expects some credit customers will not pay and reduces trade receivables by 3%."

* Concept: **Prudence.**
* Explanation: trade receivables are not overstated, so profit and assets are not overstated.

## 8. Common mistakes

* Mixing up **realisation** (when to record revenue) and **accruals** (matching income and expenses to a period).
* Saying "prudence means understating profit on purpose". It does not. It means do not **overstate**.
* Naming a concept without explaining how it applies.
